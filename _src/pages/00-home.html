Every place data moves or rests.

DialectForge gives two machines a private language that changes every two minutes. Anything that doesn't speak today's language is designed to be dropped. I've been applying that one idea to networks, storage, an operating system, and as demos and designs to radios, cars and a bank ledger. None of it has been independently tested yet.

§ 01 The idea

Two machines invent a language, use it for two minutes, then throw it away.

Most secure connections pick a well-known protocol and lock it down. That works, but a well-known protocol has a well-known shape, and anyone watching the wire can spot that shape, block it, or aim at it.

DialectForge works the other way. Both ends start from a shared random seed and work out a dialect: how identifiers are remapped, how payloads are scrambled, what the frames look like. Every 120 seconds they both move to the next dialect without having to talk about it. A frame that isn't in the current dialect isn't treated as a bad message. It's treated as noise and dropped. That's the design; it hasn't been independently tested yet.

It's its own protocol, not a wrapper around WireGuard or OpenVPN. And it doesn't care what carries it: UDP, WiFi, Bluetooth, a CAN bus, a disk block, or a page of memory.

It's like changing the locks every two minutes, and the new key is cut the same way on both sides without anyone handing it over.

§ 02 How it works

The short version, in four steps

  1. Seed

    One node makes a 256-bit random seed from hardware entropy and shares it with its peers over a protected channel. The design target is under 50 ms.

  2. Propose

    Each node's negotiation agent turns the seed into proposals: how to remap IDs, how to scramble payloads. They trade proposals for 3 to 5 rounds.

  3. Agree

    Proposals are combined (XOR consensus) into one shared dialect. A quorum of more than 70% has to agree before traffic flows.

  4. Refresh & reject

    On a timer, every 120 s by default, the dialect moves forward from the seed. Both sides know the next one already. Frames that don't fit the current dialect are dropped, and repeated misses raise an anomaly score that can lock things down and force a fresh seed.

Think of two devices inventing their own secret language every two minutes. If someone slips a fake message in, it gets thrown out, because they don't know today's language.

§ 03 Applied

One protocol, applied domain by domain

Same seed, same negotiation, same refresh, same rule about foreign frames. What changes is what gets remapped: an IP flow, a MAC address, a CAN ID, a disk sector, a memory page.

§ 04 Flagship
DFoS v1.0 “Troposphere”

An experimental operating system built around the protocol.

DFoS is a fork of the Linux kernel with DialectForge as a kernel subsystem. Every process gets its own dialect seed. Memory pages are encrypted with keys derived from it, and the filesystem speaks dialect natively. It comes with demo attack scripts I wrote. Its security hasn't been independently tested or reviewed, and I'm not claiming it resists any particular attack. Once the source is out, I'd welcome review.

§ 06 Who built it

A carpenter who built a protocol.

I'm John Martin, a fourth-generation carpenter from Michigan. I taught myself to build software the same way I learned to build houses: measure, cut, test the fit, and do it again until it's square. DialectForge started as one question: what if a connection didn't have a shape anyone could recognize? It turned into a protocol, and then I kept finding places to use it.

I also built a few iOS apps and LiveFlyer, a web app that makes gift-triggered animations and overlays for TikTok LIVE streamers. They're how I fund the work on DialectForge. LiveFlyer isn't open source.

I'll tell you what's working, what's in progress and what's still an idea. Where I say something is designed to resist an attack, that's the goal and the reason it's built that way, not a promise. That's what the open source release and outside review are for.
